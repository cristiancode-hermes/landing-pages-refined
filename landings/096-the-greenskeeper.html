Founded 1892 · Above the firth, an hour north of the city

Eighteen holes, one greenkeeper's stubborn century.

The Greenskeeper is a members' links that has never once been in a hurry. The fairways follow the land as it was found in 1892, the rough is honest, and the tenth tee still waits for the morning fog to lift — as it has every day for a hundred and thirty-three years.

Four holes people write letters about

Members argue about the best hole the way other families argue about wills. These four come up most often.

4

The Pulpit

Par 3 · 178 yards · played from a tee above the tide line

Hit it pure or hit it into the firth; there is rarely a third option. The green sits in a bowl of dunes that amplifies applause from the fifth tee, which members are known to provide ironically.

8

The End of the World

Par 5 · 561 yards · the furthest point from the clubhouse

Three honest shots into the prevailing wind, and the last of them is blind over a ridge marked by a white stone placed there in 1903. The stone has a name. Ask the starter.

11

The Loneliest Walk

Par 4 · 438 yards · no other hole in sight

For ten minutes you cannot see another golfer, another green, or any proof the clubhouse exists. Members play it in silence by unspoken agreement. Visitors usually understand by the second shot.

18

Homeward

Par 4 · 392 yards · the fairway tilts toward the clubhouse windows

Every approach is played in front of whoever is having lunch. The dining room has witnessed more shanked eight-irons than any teaching professional in the district, and is less forgiving about them.

Founded by eleven men and a borrowed mower

In March 1892, eleven merchants from the town leased “the grazing above the shore” for one pound a year and appointed a greenkeeper, Hamish Dunbar, who outlasted them all. The original nine holes were cut with a mower borrowed from the cricket club, which wanted it back every Saturday — the reason, tradition holds, that golf here has always started early.

The clubhouse went up in 1907 with timber from a wrecked coastal trader; the bar top is still the ship's hatch cover, and the ring marks are considered part of the finish. The club has had six greenkeepers in 133 years. The current one, Eilidh Dunbar, is the fourth of that name.

“We do not restore the course. We simply never let it become anything else.”

— Eilidh Dunbar, greenkeeper since 2009

The halfway house

A stone hut at the turn, run by Mrs. Lennox since 1998. Cash and honesty box; the card machine is broken in a way nobody intends to fix. Prices have risen twice in twenty-seven years, both times under protest — hers.

Visitors, plainly

We are a members' club that likes guests, in that order. Here is exactly how it works:

Visitor tee times are Tuesdays and Thursdays after 13:00, and Sundays after 15:00. That is the whole window, and it does not widen in summer.

The green fee is £95, including a plate in the dining room afterwards. Book by telephone — the pro shop answers between 8:00 and 17:00, and a person picks up.

Four hours fifteen is the expected pace. If your fourball needs five, the members behind you will let you know, politely and at length.

No handicap certificate is required, but the rough does not care what you tell the starter.

Membership, honestly

3 years,
4 months

That is the current wait for full membership, and it is a real number, updated each quarter — not a marketing pause. The list moves when members move away or, more often, when the dining room loses one.

Joining fee £850, then £1,240 a year, all golf included. There is no accelerated route, no corporate tier, and the committee reads every application in the order it arrived. Letters of introduction help; cheques attached to them do not.

Waiting-list registration is £25, refunded in full if you withdraw. About forty people a year do, usually because they have moved to somewhere with a shorter list and worse golf.
