Two centuries, one postcode.

The stillhouse has flooded twice, burned once, and changed hands only through inheritance. What follows is the short version — the tour takes three hours.

  1. 1824

    The first licence

    Eilidh Grant, widow, farmer, and previously an unlicensed distiller of some local renown, takes out licence No. 117 and names the place for the flat glen it sits in.

  2. 1861

    The Great Flood

    The Ailie takes the malting floor and 400 casks. Thirty-one are recovered downstream; one, salvaged intact, is bottled in 1911 as the "Flood Cask" and still appears at auction.

  3. 1923

    Warehouse No. 3

    Built from whinstone and slate, earthen-floored, deliberately damp. Every cask we've laid down since has slept here. The angel's share runs to 2% a year and we begrudge none of it.

  4. 1979

    The quiet years

    The industry contracts; Lowlands refuses to sell. Production drops to one day a week rather than one drop of bought-in spirit. The ledger from that decade is on display in the tasting room.

  5. 2024

    The bicentenary

    Two hundred years, sixth generation of Grants at the mash tun. We marked it by doing exactly what we did the day before: mashing in at six, cutting spirit at noon.

Come and see it working.

Tours run because the distillery runs — not the other way round. You'll see the mash tun mid-mash, taste new-make off the safe, and walk Warehouse No. 3 with the person who actually works in it.
