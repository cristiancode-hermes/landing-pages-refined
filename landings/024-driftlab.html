The legs

D-7 runs thirteen months in four legs. Crews rotate by icebreaker; the floe does not. Dates are plan-of-record — the ice edits the plan, and we log the edits.

The science program

Three work packages, one floe. Everything is cross-referenced to the drift track so a water sample and a snow pit can be placed on the same map, hour for hour.

WP-1 · GLACIOLOGY

Ice core archive

A 200 m core line across the floe, read layer by layer back to the 1960s. Each core is split on site: half analyzed in the cold lab at −20 °C, half flown home untouched.

CORES LOGGED TO DATE: 312 · OLDEST LAYER: 1964

WP-2 · OCEANOGRAPHY

Ocean moorings

Four moorings hang beneath the floe to 800 m, listening for warm Atlantic water sneaking north under the ice. Serviced by divers in dry suits, because robots hate the cold more than we do.

DEEPEST SENSOR: 812 M · WARMEST INTRUSION: +1.3 °C

WP-3 · ATMOSPHERE

Aurora optics

Three all-sky cameras and a photometer run every dark hour of the polar night, pairing auroral displays with the camp's magnetometer. The sky talks; we transcribe.

NIGHTS IMAGED: 121 · STRONGEST STORM: KP 7, 9 FEB

Read the ice, page by page.

Every winter the pack freezes a new layer; every summer it signs the margin. A core from this floe is a diary the ocean kept without being asked — ash from a Siberian fire season, the salt of a bad storm, the first spring melt spelled out in bubbles.

Driftlab exists because satellites see the ice from above and models guess at it from afar. Somebody has to stand on it and take its temperature. For thirteen months, that's us.
