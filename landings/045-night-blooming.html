Some flowers wait for the dark.

When the day visitors leave, the garden changes shift. Five species open only after sundown — and for eleven weekends each summer, you’re invited to watch it happen by lantern light.

The five that bloom at night

Each one keeps its own hour. Our docents carry red lanterns and know them all by scent before sight.

  • Moonflower

    Ipomoea alba

    Unfurls in under two minutes — fast enough to watch with the naked eye. Smells faintly of almond soap.

    Opens 21:10, North Arbor

  • Queen of the Night

    Selenicereus grandiflorus

    One night a year per flower. Ours opened for eleven hours last July and four hundred people came to see it.

    Opens ~23:00, Cactus House

  • Evening Primrose

    Oenothera biennis

    A soft yellow lamp along the gravel paths. Hawkmoths arrive within minutes of the first petal moving.

    Opens 20:45, Meadow Walk

  • Angel’s Trumpet

    Brugmansia suaveolens

    Hangs like a dropped handkerchief and perfumes the whole glasshouse. Beautiful, poisonous, admired from the path.

    Opens 21:30, Glasshouse

  • Night Jasmine

    Cestrum nocturnum

    The smallest flowers of the five, and the loudest scent. You will smell the gate arch before you see it.

    Opens 22:00, Gate Arch

The quiet rules

The garden at night belongs to its pollinators. We are the guests. These five rules keep it that way — break one kindly and we’ll remind you; break one twice and we’ll walk you to the gate.

  • Voices at library level. Sound carries strangely at night. Whispering is allowed to be wonderful.
  • No flash, ever. Red-filtered torches are handed out at the gate; phone flashes confuse the hawkmoths’ flight.
  • Stay on the lit paths. The beds look still, but half the garden is awake and walking around down there.
  • Touch nothing that’s open. A single fingerprint can close a moonflower for the night. Look, lean, don’t pick.
  • Phones on night mode, screens down. Your eyes need twenty minutes to truly see. Give them the twenty minutes.
