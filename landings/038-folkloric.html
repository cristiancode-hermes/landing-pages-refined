How a commission works

From first letter to finished cloth is usually ten to fourteen weeks. Hand work refuses to be rushed, and we've stopped apologizing for that.

  1. The letter

    Write to us — actual email is fine — about the piece: a blanket, a length of suiting, napkins for a wedding. Tell us what it's for and what it should feel like.

  2. Yarn & color

    We send physical swatch cards and dyed yarn samples by post. You decide with your hands, not a screen. This round trips as many times as it needs to.

  3. The warp

    We dress the loom and send a photo of the first woven inches for your approval. After this point the structure is set — color talk is over, weaving begins.

  4. Cloth & care

    Off the loom, washed, pressed, hemmed, and shipped with a care card and a small length of your own yarn for future mends. Mending is free, forever.

The natural-dye library

Everything we color starts as a plant, an insect, or a kitchen scrap. These are the six baths we keep in regular rotation — hover a swatch to see it the way it catches morning light in the dye room.

Madder

Rubia tinctorum. Three-year-old roots give the deep brick red of old barns. Our oldest recipe — 212 batches logged since 2014.

Indigo

Fermented leaf vat, fed on Fridays. The only blue that was ever worth the trouble. Smells terrible, behaves beautifully.

Weld

Reseda luteola. The clear, ringing yellow medieval dyers called "the queen." Grown in the studio garden, harvested in August.

Black walnut

Hulls gathered from the neighbor's tree each October, in exchange for a woven runner. No mordant needed — it dyes on its own terms.

Cochineal

The only import we allow. Seventy thousand insects to the kilo, and worth every one for that impossible carmine.

Onion skins

Saved from the studio kitchen all winter. A soft bronze that surprises everyone — proof the best dyes are sometimes free.
