The photographer who waited eleven years for one frame

By Mireille Okafor, words · Tomás Reyes, the eleven-year photograph

Every October for eleven years, Tomás Reyes drove the same four hundred kilometers to a salt flat that floods for perhaps nine days a year, if it floods at all. He carried the same camera, the same two lenses, and a camp stool he now describes, without embarrassment, as the most important piece of equipment he owns. He was waiting for a reflection that exists only when the water is two centimeters deep, the wind is under four knots, and the sun is eight degrees from the horizon. In 2017 the water came and the wind did not stop. In 2019 the wind stopped and the water did not come.

What arrived last March was forty minutes of conditions so precise that Reyes made only six exposures. "Digital made us greedy," he told me, sitting on the famous stool. "When a frame costs you eleven years, you stop taking pictures of things that almost matter." The photograph that opens this issue — a horizon doubled so perfectly that the caption writer gave up on orienting it — was exposure four. He knew it in the moment, he says, the way you know a door has closed behind you.

This issue is built around a simple, unfashionable proposition: that photography's crisis is not technological but temporal. We make more photographs in a day than the twentieth century made in a decade, and we have forgotten what the medium is actually for — which is not to capture time, but to spend it, deliberately, in one place, looking. Inside: five photographers who measure projects in years, a defense of the contact sheet, and an obituary for the phrase "just shooting."

The people in this issue

  • Mireille OkaforEditor-at-large, words

    Former picture editor at two dailies she would rather not name. Writes about slowness, sequencing, and the moral weight of the delete key. Lives in Marseille with a darkroom she uses twice a year, on principle.

  • Tomás ReyesCover photographer

    Eleven years on one salt flat. His work is held by three national collections and one very patient gallerist in Lisbon. This is his first interview in six years, granted on the condition we print no portrait.

  • Ana LindqvistContributing writer

    Writes the issue's defense of the contact sheet after cataloguing her late mentor's archive — 40,000 frames, 312 keepers, one masterpiece he never printed. She argues the rejects were the real autobiography.
