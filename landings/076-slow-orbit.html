Six hours, door to door.

Vesper I is a lifting-body spaceplane built for loiter, not sprint. She climbs gently, holds the edge of space for two full hours, and lands on the runway she left.

T+0:00

Launch

Wheels up from Spaceport Meseta at dawn. The carrier climb is airliner-smooth; most passengers read, or try to.

T+0:42

Ignition

Release at 15 km, then 80 seconds of rocket burn — 3.2 g pressed into your couch, then abrupt, ringing silence.

T+1:50

Apogee

110 km. The seat harness releases, the window covers open, and the cabin goes very quiet in a different way.

T+2:00–4:00

The overview

Two hours of free float at the window. A full night side, a sunrise, a day side — the whole disc, with time to actually look.

T+4:10

Re-entry

A long feathered glide, gentle enough to hold a camera steady. The sky comes back one shade of blue at a time.

T+5:55

Wheels down

Runway landing back at Meseta. Champagne is optional. Sitting on the tarmac for a minute is not — nobody wants to stand up yet.

Flights operate Thursday through Sunday, weather permitting. We delay for wind without apology; the refund policy is below and it is generous.

What two hours buys you.

Astronauts have a name for it — the overview effect, the cognitive shift that comes from seeing the planet as one thing. Most suborbital flights are too brief for it to land. Ours is not.

“The first twenty minutes I took photographs. Then I stopped, because the camera kept winning. I spent the rest of the time just looking, and I have been quietly reorganizing my life ever since. Nobody warns you it’s a before-and-after line.”

The training week, walked through.

Five days at Spaceport Meseta before you fly. It is not a formality, and it is not boot camp. It is the difference between enduring your flight and inhabiting it.

Day one

Medical baseline, cabin orientation, and the slow tour of Vesper I — every handle, every procedure, until the cockpit feels borrowed rather than alien.

Day two

Centrifuge. Two runs to 3.2 g so launch-day acceleration is a familiar weight, not a surprise. Most people call this the fun part by the second run.

Day three

Zero-g parabolas in the trainer aircraft. How to move, how to not move, and how to hold a camera steady while floating — the skill you’ll thank us for.

Day four

Earth-observation briefing with our planetary scientists: what you’ll see, orbit by orbit, window by window. Bring your questions about home.

Day five

Full dress rehearsal in the cabin, then rest. Dinner with your five crewmates. Early night; dawn comes at 4:40.

The honest medical page.

We would rather lose a booking than a passenger. Here is exactly what flight asks of a body, and who we have to turn away.

What your body will do

  • 3.2 g for about 80 seconds at ignition — like a firm hand on the chest, trained for on day two.
  • Roughly four hours of weightlessness. About one in three passengers feels queasy in the first hour; medication is available and normal.
  • Cabin altitude equivalent of 2,400 m during the carrier climb, sea-level pressure at apogee.
  • Ages 18 to 79 have flown with us. The oldest passenger to date is 81, after a longer medical review.

Who we cannot fly

  • Uncontrolled hypertension, recent cardiac surgery, or a stroke within the past two years.
  • Pregnancy, at any stage — the data simply doesn’t exist, so the answer is no.
  • Severe osteoporosis or recent spinal surgery; re-entry loads are gentle but real.
  • Anyone our flight surgeon flags and cannot clear. The deposit is refunded in full, always.
