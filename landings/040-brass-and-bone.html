What we take on

Twelve pieces a season means we say no often, and in writing. Here is where our benches earn their keep — and where they don't.

Case goods
Secretaries, highboys, campaign chests, cellarettes. Failed drawer runners, lifting veneer, structural repairs to carcasses that have moved house too many times.
Seating
Pre-1900 frames re-railed and re-glued, rush and cane seats rewoven, upholstery stripped to the frame and rebuilt in traditional materials. We keep the old horsehair when it's sound.
Clocks & instruments
Cases only — we conserve the cabinet while a horologist we trust minds the movement. We do not service movements ourselves, and we won't pretend otherwise.
What we decline
Wicker, painted folk pieces best left to a paintings conservator, and anything still under a living maker's warranty. Declining is free, and we will tell you who to call instead.

If a piece is younger than our apprentices, it is probably a repair, not a conservation — we can recommend a good cabinetmaker.

The assessment visit

Every piece enters the shop the same way, whether it's a family Bible box or a museum's high chest. Three steps, and you're in control of all of them.

  1. The visit

    We come to you, within sixty miles of Hudson. $120, credited in full against the work. We look, we measure, we open every drawer and turn the piece over if you'll let us. Forty-five minutes, usually less.

  2. The report

    Within ten days you receive a written condition report: photographs, structural findings, finish history as far as we can read it, and the options — conserve, restore, or leave alone — with honest costs for each.

  3. The decision

    Yours, entirely. About a third of the reports we write end with our recommendation to do nothing at all. That answer costs you nothing beyond the visit, and we put the reasoning in writing.

The green cabinet, as found and as it left

A Chippendale-style corner cupboard came to us last autumn with three generations of paint, a door that hadn't closed since the Eisenhower administration, and original brass under a century of neglect.

Eight weeks: paint read layer by layer before any was lifted, hinges re-pinned, the door re-hung true. The brass was cleaned to a soft glow — not polished to a mirror, because a mirror finish on 1780s hardware is a lie.

Drag the handle. Left, the cupboard as it arrived. Right, the morning it went home. Every hour is in the written record that went with it.

Conservation is not restoration, and the difference matters.

We conserve when the finish is the story

Original shellac, honest wear at the drawer pulls, a burn mark from 1911 that is part of the record — these are not damage. They are evidence. We stabilize, we clean, we protect, and we stop there.

We restore when the piece can't be used

Failed joints, active beetle, a veneer field too far gone to read. Then we rebuild — in reversible materials, documented at every step, so a future conservator can undo anything we did.

If a dealer tells you restoration always adds value, find another dealer. Sometimes the most valuable thing we do is nothing.
